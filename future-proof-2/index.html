Spec Wars

"8 cores! 4 GHz! 2 TB!" What do the numbers on the box actually mean, and why doesn't the biggest number always win? Play Top Trumps with real machines, race some storage, then clean up a messy database.

Clock speed, cores, RAM, storageBottlenecksStorage unitsData cleaning
3.1 · Decode the box

What the numbers mean

Clock speed (GHz)

How many steps the processor takes each second. 1 GHz = 1 billion ticks a second. Faster ticks = more work, but also more heat.

Cores

Extra workers inside one processor. 8 cores help only if the job can be split up, like drawing lots of pixels or running several apps.

RAM (GB)

The size of the desk. More RAM lets you keep more apps open without slowing down.

Storage (GB, TB)

The filing cabinet. How much it holds matters, and so does how fast it is: an SSD can be about 50 times faster than a hard disk.

Graphics chip (GPU)

Thousands of small cores that work at the same time. Great for games, video and AI.

The GHz paradox. From the ZX Spectrum (1982) to Apple's M5 chip (2025), clock speed went up about 1,300 times. Memory went up about 250,000 times. Clock speeds got stuck around 4–5 GHz in the mid-2000s because chips got too hot. So engineers added more cores and bigger caches instead. You'll find out more in Mission 8.

3.4 · Size order

Bits, bytes and terabytes

8 bits = 1 byte. Then each step up is about 1,000 times bigger: kilobyte (KB) → megabyte (MB) → gigabyte (GB) → terabyte (TB). (Strictly, computers often count in 1,024s.) Put these in order from smallest to largest.

How many floppy disks?

A 1990s floppy disk held 1.44 MB. IBM's first hard disk (1956) held 3.75 MB and weighed almost a tonne.

4.3 · Fact check

4.4 · Patterns

Why do predictions go wrong?

In 1978, Roy Amara noticed something that became known as Amara's Law: we usually overestimate what a new technology will do in the short run, and underestimate what it will do in the long run.

Right gadget, wrong date

Shopping and banking from home worked on Prestel in 1979. Hardly anyone used it. It took the web, 20 years later, to make it normal.

Gadgets, not habits

People imagined robot maids and glowing ceilings. Hardly anyone predicted we'd use phones as cameras, wallets and maps, or work from home.

Judging by today's limits

Experts said the internet would collapse and the iPhone would flop, because they judged them by what existed then.

Trends beat dreams

Moore measured a real trend in chips and was right for decades. Flying cars came from imagination and are still waiting.

Good stories spread

Made-up quotes survive because they are funny, not because they are true.

5.1 · Study the style

How 1980s computer adverts worked

Home computer adverts in UK magazines usually shouted the price, bragged about memory in "K" and made big claims. Many were sold by post: you cut out a coupon and waited weeks.

The 80s advert checklist

  • A big headline, often with the price
  • Specs as boasts: "48K!", "16 colours!"
  • A huge claim ("the world's best… for under £500")
  • The product on a plain black or white background
  • Mail order: "allow 28 days for delivery"
  • A pun, slogan or jingle line
  • Only words people knew in 1985

Sinclair's "28 days delivery" promise got it into trouble with the Advertising Standards Authority: some buyers waited 8–12 weeks.

    6.5 · Case file · Scotland

    The day Shetland went offline

    In October 2022, the undersea cables connecting Shetland to the mainland and to the Faroe Islands were damaged. Broadband, phones and mobile signal went down across the islands. Police declared a major incident.

    Engineers set up temporary links using satellite and wireless backup, and the cables were repaired within days.

    Today Scotland's R100 programme is laying 16 new undersea fibre cables to connect 15 islands, from Iona to Unst.

    Talk to the Bit

    The BBC micro:bit has a radio built in. It is very simple: it shouts messages to every micro:bit on the same channel. No addresses, no secrets, no routing. That means you get to invent the rules that real networks use.

    Two devices communicatingProtocols you buildSensors and feedback
    7.1 · Know your radio

    What the micro:bit radio can (and can't) do

    Groups 0–255

    Like walkie-talkie channels. A micro:bit only hears others in the same group.

    Tiny packets

    A text message can be up to 19 characters. Longer ones must be split up.

    No addresses

    Every message goes to everyone in the group. If you want "to" and "from", you add them yourself.

    No secrets

    Nothing is encrypted. Anyone on your group can read your messages.

    Range

    About 20 m normally. Up to about 70 m in open space at full power (real micro:bits only).

    8.2 · Chart
    Moore's Law, and where it hit a wall
    
  
    In 1965 Gordon Moore predicted that the number of tiny switches (transistors) on a chip would keep doubling. In 1975 he said every two years. The first microprocessor (1971) had 2,300. Look at the chart, then switch to a log scale, where every grid line is 10 times the one below. On a log scale, a straight line means steady doubling.
    
  
    
  
    
  
    Predict before you peek
    The 2024 dots are hidden. Using the trend on the log scale, guess how many transistors the biggest single AI chip had in 2024.
    
    
    
    
    
  
    The wall. Switch to clock speed. Around 2005, clock speeds stopped climbing at about 4 GHz. Tiny transistors leaked electricity and got too hot. Transistor counts kept growing, so engineers spent them on more cores, bigger caches and graphics and AI chips instead. A new chip factory now costs about $33 billion, and only a handful of companies can make the newest chips. Moore's Law is an observation, not a law of nature, and it is slowing.
