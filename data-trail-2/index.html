PART 1 OF 6 · ABOUT 2 LESSONS

Your data trail

Before an AI can influence you, it needs data. Here you find out what is collected, how websites follow you around, and the difference between what you share and what is guessed about you.

1.1 · READ AND DISCUSS · 10 MIN

Where does the data come from?

Most apps you use are free because they make money from advertising. Advertisers pay more when they can reach exactly the right people, so apps try to know as much about you as possible.

Data comes from three places: the app itself (what you watch, like, type and buy), your device (location, phone model, battery level, which other apps are installed) and other websites that share data through trackers.

Some companies, called data brokers, collect data from many sources and sell profiles to other businesses. You will probably never visit their websites, but they may have a file on your household.

Cookie
A small file a website stores in your browser so it recognises you next time.
Third-party cookie
A cookie from a company that isn’t the site you’re on, usually an advertiser.
Tracking pixel
An invisible image in a web page or email that reports when you opened it.
Device ID
A code that identifies your phone to advertisers across many apps.
Profile
Everything a company has collected or guessed about you, linked together.
Data broker
A business that buys, combines and sells people’s data.
Volunteered data
Data you choose to give, like a username, a post or a date of birth.
Captured data
Data collected automatically as you use a device or app, like location or watch time.

PART 2 OF 6 · ABOUT 2 LESSONS

How AI learns you

Recommendation systems are AI that decide what you see next. They learn from your behaviour and from people who behave like you. Here you see the loop from the inside.

2.1 · EXPLAINER · 10 MIN

The engagement loop

A recommendation system repeats four steps millions of times a day. It collects data about you, predicts how likely you are to engage with each possible post, acts by showing the posts with the highest scores, then measures your reaction and adds that to the data.

The key question is what the system is told to aim for. If the goal is “keep people watching”, it will learn whatever works, including content that makes people angry, anxious or unable to stop. Nobody has to program that on purpose. The AI finds it because it works.

There are two main ways systems choose: content-based (more of what you already liked) and collaborative filtering (what people similar to you liked). Most real apps combine both, along with thousands of other signals.

2.4 · COMPARE · 15 MIN

Rules or data? Two ways to build a system

Not every system learns. A rule-based system follows instructions a person wrote, so the same input always gives the same output. A data-driven system learns patterns from data, so its outputs adapt and can change. Ask both systems to choose a post for the same person and compare.

Rule-based feed

IF age < 16 AND time is after 22:00 show a calm post ELSE IF user follows a topic show newest post from that topic ELSE show today’s most popular post

Data-driven feed

Learned from millions of past swipes which post each kind of person is most likely to watch next. Nobody wrote the rules; the model found them in the data.

Who decides the logic?A programmer writes itThe model learns it from training data

When AI gets it wrong: bias

An AI is only as fair as the data it learns from. If the past was unfair, a system trained on the past can repeat that unfairness automatically, at huge scale, while looking neutral.

4.2 · TRAIN, TEST, FIX · 20 MIN

GradeBot: is it judging you, or your school?

Exams are cancelled. GradeBot must predict whether each pupil deserves an A or B, using past pupils’ results as training data. This time the problem is different from CV-Bot, and so are the fixes.

This really happened · Scotland, August 2020

When exams were cancelled during the pandemic, the SQA adjusted teachers’ estimated grades using each school’s past results. About 125,000 estimates were lowered. The Higher pass rate for pupils from the most deprived areas was cut by 15.2 percentage points, compared with 6.9 for the least deprived. After pupils protested, the Scottish Government reversed the downgrades and used teacher estimates instead.

This was a statistical method, not machine learning, but it made the same mistake GradeBot does: judging pupils partly by their school’s past. Sources: The Scotsman · LBC

Investigate: Try each fix on its own, then together. Why didn’t removing the school name work on its own? Why didn’t more data help this time, when it helped CV-Bot? Is it ever fair to use information about a group to judge one person?

5.2 · THE BIGGER PICTURE · 20 MIN

Who does AI work for, and what does it cost?

The same data and AI that target adverts can also make technology work better for more people. But it all runs in data centres that need energy and water. Both sides matter when you judge a system.

AI that adapts to different users

AI can personalise more than adverts. It can adapt how information reaches people.

  • Automatic captions help people who are Deaf or hard of hearing.
  • Image descriptions help blind and partially sighted people using screen readers.
  • Text-to-speech and reading tools can support people with dyslexia.
  • Live translation helps people who are still learning a language.
  • Voice control helps people with limited movement use devices.

But an AI only works well for the people its training data represents. Speech recognition has often been less accurate for some accents and dialects, and smaller languages such as Gaelic usually get fewer tools. This is the bias problem from Part 4 again.

Design for one user

The energy cost

Every recommendation, search and AI answer is calculated on computers in a data centre. The International Energy Agency (IEA) estimates:

2024
~415 TWh
2030
~945 TWh (projected)
  • In 2024 data centres used about 1.5% of the world’s electricity.
  • By 2030 that is expected to more than double, to slightly more than the whole of Japan uses today. AI is a major reason.
  • Many data centres also use large amounts of water for cooling.

Closer to home

In January 2026 the UK Government announced an AI Growth Zone in Airdrie, North Lanarkshire, delivered by DataVita with CoreWeave. Plans include more than 500MW of on-site power generation prioritising renewables, and capturing waste heat from the data centres, possibly to supply University Hospital Monklands. Industry body techUK raised concerns about energy costs and grid connections.

Sources: IEA, Energy and AI · techUK on the Lanarkshire AI Growth Zone

Discuss: Is it worth using energy so an app can personalise your feed? What about personalised captions or reading support? Should AI that helps accessibility be treated differently from AI that sells adverts?
PART 6 OF 6 · ABOUT 1 LESSON

Your rights and your choices

In the UK, the law gives you real rights over your data, with extra protections for children. Knowing them turns you from a data source into someone who can push back.

6.1 · KNOW YOUR RIGHTS · 15 MIN

What the law says

These come mainly from UK GDPR and the ICO’s Children’s Code (Age Appropriate Design Code). The Online Safety Act adds duties on platforms to protect under-18s from harmful content.

Right of accessAsk any organisation for a copy of the data it holds about you.Usually free, with a reply within one month.
Right to erasureAsk for your data to be deleted in many situations.Especially strong for data collected when you were a child.
Right to objectSay no to your data being used for direct marketing.Companies must stop when you object to marketing.
Right to be informedOrganisations must explain clearly what they collect and why.For children, in language they can understand.
Automated decisionsProtections when important decisions are made about you by computer alone.Think CV-Bot: you can ask for a human to review.
Age 13In the UK, apps need a parent or carer’s consent to use under-13s’ data on the basis of consent.Why TikTok was fined (Case file 5).
High privacy by defaultThe Children’s Code expects settings for under-18s to start private.Location off and profiling off unless there’s a good reason.
No harmful nudgesThe Children’s Code says services shouldn’t use nudge techniques that push children to weaken their privacy.Links to the dark patterns in Part 3.

FOR TEACHERS

Teacher notes

Learning intentions

  • Explain how given, observed and inferred data are collected online, including by third-party trackers.
  • Describe how recommendation systems (content-based and collaborative filtering) predict and influence behaviour.
  • Identify persuasive and deceptive design (dark patterns).
  • Explain how biased training data produces biased models, including through proxy variables, and why testing on unseen data matters.
  • Evaluate real cases using fairness, consent, transparency and regulation (UK GDPR, Children’s Code, Online Safety Act, consumer law).

How the simulations work

Trackers: each pretend site embeds a fixed set of tracker companies; each tracker joins every visit it sees into one profile until cookies are cleared.

Feed: share +4, like +3, watch all +2, dwell up to +2, skip −1 per topic. About 75% of posts come from top topics; the rest are random exploration.

News feed: topic, tone and debate-side scores rise with each read, like, share or comment (and with time spent on a headline). In Engagement mode worrying headlines get a 1.25× boost and shocking ones 1.45×. Balanced mode favours reliable outlets and calm headlines, avoids repeating recent topics and shows the side of a debate you have seen less. All outlets and headlines are fictional.

People like you: similarity = 100% minus the average rating difference across the five rated videos. The two nearest users’ ratings are averaged to predict the three unseen videos.

CV-Bot: keyword weight = (hired − rejected) ÷ times seen. CV score = sum of weights; above 0 is shortlisted.

Safeguarding

Case file 4 refers to harmful content in teen feeds without detail; signpost pastoral support first. In the project, pupils should only investigate services they are old enough to use, never share screenshots showing personal data, and must not create accounts to test platforms. The data request letter is for practice; pupils should talk to a parent or carer before sending one.
